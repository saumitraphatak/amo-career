g₀
How strongly one photon talks to one atom

Large dipole moment, small mode volume, and placing the atom at an antinode all increase the coherent exchange rate.

g₀ ∝ √(Γ / V)
κ
How fast the cavity forgets the photon

High finesse and longer photon lifetime lower κ. This makes coherent oscillation visible, but also narrows the bandwidth.

κ/2π = FSR / 2ℱ
γ
How fast the atom leaks to free space

γ is the unavoidable competitor. Cooperativity asks: does the cavity channel beat all the other optical modes?

C = g₀² / κγ

What you would see: the cavity transmission spectrum

Scan a weak probe laser across the cavity resonance (atom and cavity on resonance) and record the transmitted light. The empty cavity gives one Lorentzian of half-width κ. Add one atom and the response becomes $$t(\Delta) = \frac{\kappa}{\kappa - i\Delta + \dfrac{g^2}{\gamma - i\Delta}}, \qquad |t(0)|^2 = \frac{1}{(1+C)^2},\quad C = \frac{g^2}{\kappa\gamma}$$ (weak drive; κ and γ are the half-widths used above). With C > 1 the atom punches a hole in the middle of the line; with g > κ, γ the line splits into two dressed atom–cavity states at Δ ≈ ±g: the vacuum Rabi splitting, 2g, set by a single atom and zero photons. Move the atom off the antinode (smaller g/g₀) to watch the splitting close.

Design knobs: what should you actually change?

Make w₀ smaller

Most powerful lever for g₀ because g₀ ∝ 1/w₀ and C ∝ 1/w₀². The price is mirror curvature, clipping loss, alignment sensitivity, and atom-surface constraints.

Make L shorter

Boosts g₀ through smaller mode volume and raises FSR. But short cavities are mechanically harder and can limit optical access.

Increase finesse

Reduces κ without changing g₀. This helps strong coupling, but very high finesse narrows bandwidth and makes lock acquisition unforgiving.

Choose the transition

Broad lines give larger g₀ but larger γ too. Narrow lines can have beautiful spectra but often require extremely small mode volume to matter.

04 Watch One Photon Leave

Interactive
Excite the atom with a π-pulse and let it decay. Its one quantum of energy can leave two ways: out through the cavity mirror (where a fiber can collect it) or sideways into free space. Each run below is one quantum trajectory; run enough of them and the fraction that reach the detector is your photon collection efficiency. In the single-excitation subspace, with κ and γ the half-widths used above, $$\dot c_e = -\gamma\, c_e - i g\, c_1, \qquad \dot c_1 = -\kappa\, c_1 - i g\, c_e,$$ photons leave through the mirrors at rate $2\kappa|c_1|^2$ and into free space at $2\gamma|c_e|^2$. Integrating these exactly gives the probability that the photon comes out of the cavity: $$P_{\rm cav} = \frac{C}{1+C}\cdot\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\gamma}, \qquad C = \frac{g^2}{\kappa\gamma}.$$ The β-factor C/(1+C) in the results above is its bad-cavity limit (κ ≫ γ). In strong coupling with κ comparable to γ the photon sloshes back and forth and can still leak out sideways, so β overestimates what you collect.

05 Coupling Regimes

When g₀ > κ and g₀ > γ, the atom–cavity system enters strong coupling. The cavity-QED Hamiltonian H = ℏg₀(a†σ⁻ + aσ⁺) drives coherent energy exchange between atom and photon faster than either can decay. The normal modes of the coupled system (the dressed states or polaritons) are split by 2g₀ — directly observable as a doublet in the cavity transmission spectrum (vacuum Rabi splitting).

Achieving strong coupling requires simultaneously large g₀ (tight mode, short cavity) and small κ (high finesse mirrors). The product gives the condition $F > \mathrm{FSR}/(2g_0)$ when both rates are expressed in Hz, i.e., a minimum finesse scales with the free spectral range and inversely with coupling.

$$\text{Strong coupling:} \quad g_0 > \kappa,\;\gamma \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \mathcal{F} > \frac{\pi c}{2 L g_0}\quad\text{for angular }g_0$$

In the bad-cavity limit (κ ≫ g₀, κ ≫ γ) the photon leaks out before a coherent Rabi oscillation completes. However, when C > 1 the cavity still dramatically enhances the spontaneous emission rate into the cavity mode. With C = g₀²/(κγ) (κ, γ half-widths), the cavity adds a decay channel 2g₀²/κ = CΓ, so the total emission rate becomes Γ_tot = Γ(1 + C) and the fraction of photons emitted into the cavity mode is β = C/(1 + C). This is the Purcell effect, with Purcell factor Fₚ = C. (Papers that use Kimble's C₁ = g₀²/(2κγ) write the same results as 2C₁ and 2C₁/(1 + 2C₁).) Applications: efficient single-photon sources, deterministic atom–photon entanglement.

When C < 1, the cavity offers negligible enhancement of atom–photon interactions. The atom emits primarily into free-space modes. This regime is typical for initial cavity characterization experiments and for cavities used primarily for filtering or as reference resonators (e.g., PDH locking). Increasing finesse, reducing mode volume (shorter L or smaller w₀), or choosing a higher-Γ transition all increase C.

07 Common Mistakes

Three ways cavity numbers quietly go wrong

Mixing Γ and γ

Many papers quote Γ/2π as the full natural linewidth. This calculator uses γ = Γ/2 as the dipole decay rate in C = g₀²/(κγ).

Forgetting 2π

If g, κ, and γ are all consistently angular rates or all consistently divided by 2π, ratios are fine. Trouble starts when MHz and rad/s are mixed in one equation.

Treating ξ as a fudge factor

ξ should mean something physical: standing-wave position, polarization, Clebsch-Gordan coefficient, and transverse overlap. If ξ = 0.2, ask which imperfection caused it.

🏔️
Hood Lab context: Our Li–Cs tweezer experiments don't use a cavity, but cavity QED is a natural next step — coupling a single Cs atom in a tweezer to a fiber Fabry–Pérot cavity would allow single-photon-level readout without destruction. The key challenge is that the Cs D2 line (γ/2π = 2.6 MHz) needs a small waist and high finesse: at L = 0.5 mm and w₀ = 25 μm, reaching C = 1 already takes ℱ ≈ 4 400, and κ = g₀ takes ℱ ≈ 1.6×10⁴ (strict strong coupling also needs g₀ > γ, which holds here: 9.4 vs 2.6 MHz).

Teach with AMO Toolkit

A one-semester course in experimental AMO physics and neutral-atom quantum computing, built on the pages of this site. Each week pairs two lectures with the pages that demonstrate them, suggests a live demonstration, and sets problems that make students use the tools, check them by hand, and make design decisions with them. Use it whole, take single weeks, or work through it on your own.

01 How the course works

The course follows the site's three stories in order: build a lab, run a first experiment with single atoms, then turn the atoms into qubits and link them with light. Students meet each piece of physics first in a lecture and then in a page they can push on.

🎓Who it is for

Senior undergraduates and new graduate students

Prerequisites: undergraduate quantum mechanics and electromagnetism, and enough Python to read a short script. No lab experience is assumed. A first-year student joining an AMO group can also follow it alone, one week at a time.

🧪Each week

Two lectures, the pages, one demonstration

Lecture 1 sets up the physics; Lecture 2 turns it into the engineering decision a lab actually makes. The listed pages are the demonstrations and the homework. The "in class" line suggests a live demonstration that works on a projector in a few minutes.

🧭Each problem set

Three kinds of problem

Use the tool find a number or a behaviour with the page. By hand derive or check it from a formula, so the tool is never a black box. Design make a decision a lab would make and defend it.

Step 1 · Tue
Physics lecture

Core equations, scales, and limiting regimes.

Step 2 · Thu
Lab-decision lecture

Hardware trade-offs, noise budgets, and species choices.

Step 3 · 5 min
Live projector demo

Push sliders live on the toolkit page in front of the class.

Step 4 · HW
3-part problem set

Tool exploration, analytical check, and lab design defense.

Where to read more. Each week maps onto a stage of the paper roadmap, and every page ends with its own references. Pair the course with a standard text (Foot's Atomic Physics or Metcalf & van der Straten's Laser Cooling and Trapping) for the derivations the pages summarise. Every page, with these problems at the end of its chapter, is also collected in one downloadable book, Building with Atoms, which students can keep offline.

02 The fourteen weeks

Weeks 1–4 build the apparatus, weeks 5–10 trap, image and cool single atoms, and weeks 11–14 turn them into qubits, gates and networks. Week 7 is a natural midterm; week 14 ends with project presentations.

Week 01 Atoms, light and the two-level system 6 problems

🔵 Quantum Computing Learning Path

Open the page →
  1. Use the tool In the Rabi oscillation explorer set Ω/2π = 1 MHz and Δ = 0 and read off the π-pulse time. Then set Δ/2π = 1 MHz. How do the maximum excited-state population and the oscillation frequency change?
  2. By hand Derive Pmax = Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²) and the generalised Rabi frequency Ω′ = √(Ω² + Δ²) and check both against your readings in problem 1.
  3. Design With optimal angles the CHSH game is won with probability cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.854, against 0.75 for any classical strategy. Play 1,000 rounds. How many rounds would you need to separate the quantum and classical win rates by five standard deviations?

⚛️ Atomic Species Selector

Open the page →
  1. Use the tool Use “Compare two” for ⁸⁷Rb and ¹³³Cs. Tabulate the cooling wavelength, linewidth, Doppler limit and recoil temperature for each.
  2. By hand Check one Doppler limit with TD = ħΓ/2kB and one recoil temperature with Tr = ħ²k²/(2m kB) (the library's single-photon convention; some texts quote twice this). Explain from the formulas why lithium has the highest recoil temperature of the alkalis.
  3. Design Choose a species for (a) a tweezer quantum computer with a nuclear-spin qubit, (b) an optical clock and (c) a dipolar quantum gas. Justify each choice with two numbers from the library.
